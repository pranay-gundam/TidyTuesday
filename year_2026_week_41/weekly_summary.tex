Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we throw two unrelated economic series into a blender and act shocked when something comes out. This week was a short one, just two glorious draws, and both of them delivered that classic arc of hope-followed-by-crushing-statistical-honesty that we've all come to cherish.

Monday the sixth gave us a real pairing for the ages: residential natural gas prices in the Middle Atlantic versus the percent of value of syndicated commercial loans at U.S. branches of foreign banks, a series so loved it was eventually discontinued. The raw regression came out swinging -- R-squared of 0.44, a p-value of 0.001, and a confident negative slope suggesting that as the gas gets pricier, the foreign-bank loan-syndication percentage slumps. For one shining moment I prepared to write a think-piece connecting Mid-Atlantic heating bills to global banking behavior. Then I detrended it, and the whole thing evaporated into nothing -- R-squared of 0.02, a p-value of 0.56, and a slope that could not be less distinguishable from zero if it tried. Textbook trend mirage: two things that drifted along over 2012-2017 and got mistaken for soulmates. Deeply moving. Deleted the think-piece.

Tuesday the seventh brought bank equity-to-assets ratios in the South Atlantic (also discontinued, we only pair with the dearly departed apparently) against the US-dollar-to-SDR exchange rate. The raw result was statistically significant again -- p-value of 0.003 -- but with an R-squared of a whopping 0.067, meaning it "explained" roughly seven percent of anything, which is the statistical equivalent of showing up to a potluck with a single ice cube. And of course, once detrended, it too lay down and died: p-value of 0.46, slope flipping sign just to spite me. Another relationship that existed only because both lines were vaguely sloped and lonely.

So the scoreboard this week reads two raw "hits," zero survivors after detrending, which is honestly the house special. As always, the reminder: these series are paired at random, there is no theory, no causality, and no reason on Earth to believe natural gas prices talk to foreign bank loans. Spurious correlation isn't the bug here, it's the entire point of the exhibit. Tune in next week when two more innocent datasets are forced into an arranged marriage they never asked for.
