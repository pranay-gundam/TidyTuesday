Welcome back to the weekly segment where we gleefully smash random strangers from the FRED database together like action figures and ask, with a straight face, whether they're in love. As always, the answer is "no," but we run the numbers anyway because apparently this is what we do with our one wild and precious life. Four days, four pairings, and the usual parade of discontinued time series nobody has clicked on since 2017.

Monday kicked things off with Residential Natural Gas prices in the Middle Atlantic going head-to-head with the "Percent of Value of Loans Made Under Participation or Syndication for All Commercial and Industry Loans, Other Risk (Acceptable)" at foreign bank branches -- a title so long it needs its own ZIP code. The raw regression came out swinging: an R-squared of 0.44 and a p-value of 0.001, which in polite society would get you a standing ovation. Then we detrended it and the whole thing evaporated into an R-squared of 0.02 and a p-value of basically one. In other words, two things that happened to wander in the same general direction over a few years, holding hands only because they were walking down the same hallway. Textbook trend-mirage. Chef's kiss. Dunked.

Tuesday paired bank equity ratios in the South Atlantic with the dollar-to-SDR exchange rate, which gave us a technically-significant raw p-value of 0.003 attached to a gloriously pathetic R-squared of 0.067 -- explaining roughly seven percent of anything, which is the statistical equivalent of showing up to a potluck with a single grape. Detrended, it slumped to a p-value of 0.46 and gave up entirely. Wednesday, meanwhile, was the real plot twist: Hong Kong's holdings of U.S. long-term corporate bonds versus commercial carbon dioxide emissions from natural gas pipelines. The raw version was significant (p = 0.004), fine, whatever. But the detrended version STAYED significant (p = 0.003) and actually got a little stronger, flipping to a negative relationship. I am legally obligated to call this out as the rare, interesting one -- the correlation that survives having its trend ripped out. Does Hong Kong's bond portfolio secretly govern American pipeline emissions? Absolutely not. Is it fun to pretend for a second? Also absolutely not, but here we are. Thursday closed us out with two discontinued bank series shrugging at each other, posting a raw p-value of 0.17 and nobody even trying.

So the scoreboard: three trend-mirages and one genuinely stubborn correlation that I promise means nothing. Let me repeat that for the people frantically reaching for a napkin to sketch a theory: these series were paired at random, there is no causal claim, no rigorous statistical finding, and no reason to believe any of this beyond "numbers sometimes wiggle together." Spurious correlation isn't the bug here -- it's the entire genre. See you next week for more of it.
