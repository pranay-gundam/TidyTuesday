Welcome back to another thrilling installment of Correlations Nobody Asked For, where we take two economic time series that have never met, sit them down at the same table, and force them to explain their relationship to a judgmental linear regression. This week's randomly selected odd couple: the Producer Price Index for Residential Natural Gas in the Middle Atlantic, and -- deep breath -- the Percent of Value of Loans Made Under Participation or Syndication for All Commercial and Industry Loans, Other Risk (Acceptable), U.S. Branches and Agencies of Foreign Banks, which is also, I should mention, DISCONTINUED. Nothing says rigorous science like one of your variables having been quietly retired in 2017.

The raw regression came out swinging. An R-squared of 0.44, a p-value of 0.001, a confidently negative coefficient -- by the breathless standards of this project, these are practically wedding bells. "Natural gas prices go up, foreign bank syndicated loan risk percentages go down!" I can already hear the cable news chyron. Someone alert the Federal Reserve. Somebody write a thesis. The two series were apparently drifting in roughly opposite directions over their shared years, and the regression, bless its credulous little heart, decided that meant something.

And then we detrended it, and the whole romance evaporated like morning dew. Once we stripped out each series' own long-run march through time, the R-squared collapsed to a majestic 0.019, the p-value ballooned to 0.56, and the coefficient's confidence interval cheerfully straddles zero like it's got nothing to hide -- because it doesn't. In other words, the "relationship" was pure shared drift: two trends passing in the night, mistaken for love. This is the textbook trend-mirage this project exists to gently mock, and it delivered one right on schedule.

As always, the mandatory reminder: these are randomly paired series with zero theoretical reason to be related, and nothing here constitutes a causal claim, a statistical finding, or financial advice you should act on in any capacity whatsoever. Spurious correlation isn't the bug here -- it's the entire genre. This week's natural gas and discontinued foreign-bank loans were never a couple; they just happened to be walking the same direction for a while. See you next week for more statistical theater.
