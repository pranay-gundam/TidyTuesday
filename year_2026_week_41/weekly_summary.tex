Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we grab two economic time series that have never met, never will meet, and have no business being in the same spreadsheet, and then we ask them to explain each other. As always, the house rules apply: these pairs are drawn at random, no causal story is intended, and if you walk away from this convinced that natural gas prices control foreign bond holdings, that's a you problem, not a statistics problem. Spurious correlation isn't a bug here. It's the entire point.

We opened Monday with the Producer Price Index for residential natural gas in the Middle Atlantic versus the percent of syndicated loans at foreign bank branches rated "Acceptable" risk. The raw regression came out swinging with an R-squared of 0.44 and a p-value of 0.001, which in less careful hands would get a headline like "Gas Prices Secretly Rule the Banking System." And then we detrended it, and the whole thing evaporated like a politely-rated loan in a downturn: R-squared cratered to 0.02 and the p-value strolled up to 0.56. Classic trend mirage. Two series that happened to wander in roughly compatible directions, holding hands only until we took the trend away, at which point they pretended they'd never met. Beautiful. Textbook. Framed and hung in the gallery.

Tuesday served up bank equity-to-assets ratios in the South Atlantic versus the dollar-to-SDR exchange rate, and honestly this one never even made it out of the gate. Yes, the raw version was technically "significant" at p=0.003, but with an R-squared of 0.067 it was explaining roughly nothing with great confidence, which is the statistical equivalent of being extremely sure about a whisper. Detrended, it collapsed to an R-squared of 0.004 and a p-value of 0.46, so it joins Monday in the trend-mirage bin. Two for two on relationships that only exist if you squint at the long-run drift and believe really hard.

Then Wednesday actually got interesting, and I'm as uncomfortable about it as you are. Hong Kong's holdings of U.S. long-term corporate bonds against commercial carbon dioxide emissions from natural gas pipelines. The raw regression showed a positive relationship (R-squared 0.24), and here's the plot twist: when we detrended, it didn't just survive, it got slightly stronger (R-squared 0.25, p=0.003) and flipped sign to negative. That's the rare bird this project pretends to be hunting for: a relationship that holds up after we strip the trends out, which makes it harder to wave away as mere shared drift. Do I have a theory? Sure, in the sense that I can string words together: maybe years of heavier pipeline gas use loosely coincide with periods when Hong Kong investors felt differently about U.S. corporate paper. Is that theory worth the electrons it's printed on? Absolutely not. It's a tiny annual sample, two discontinued-ish datasets duct-taped together, and I would not bet a sandwich on it. But as far as this gloriously pointless exercise goes, it's the week's one genuine "huh." Tune in next week, when we will once again torture unrelated numbers until they confess to things they didn't do.
