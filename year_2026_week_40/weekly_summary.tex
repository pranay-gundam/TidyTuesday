Welcome back to the only weekly newsletter brave enough to shove two completely unrelated government spreadsheets into a regression and act surprised by the results. This week the slot machine of FRED coughed up a genuinely eclectic lineup, and I'm pleased to report that chaos remained firmly in charge. As always, the standing reminder: these pairs are chosen at random, nobody is claiming anybody causes anybody, and if you find yourself nodding along to any of the "theories" below, please go outside.

Monday kicked things off with Great Lakes wood product manufacturing GDP versus mobile cellular subscriptions across Europe and Central Asia, a pairing so natural it barely needs explaining. The raw regression shrugged -- nothing significant, a pathetic R-squared, and a p-value that wandered off to get a coffee. But then, plot twist nobody asked for: the detrended version actually turned up significant, with Midwestern lumber output mildly and negatively linked to how many phones Kazakhstan has. This is the rare, slightly unsettling case where the relationship survives the trend-stripping gauntlet, which technically makes it "better evidence of a genuine link." I will let you sit with the implications of Michigan plywood haunting Eurasian cell plans. I certainly won't be.

Tuesday delivered the week's headline act: a discontinued 1929-to-2011 measure of wage-and-salary share of national income against the number of Black or African American persons not in the labor force, and WOW did the raw regression light up like a slot machine jackpot -- R-squared of 0.89, a p-value with twenty zeros, the works. Hold the parade, though, because this is the exact trend-mirage this project exists to mock: two long series both marching in the same direction over decades will "correlate" beautifully for no reason at all. The detrended version promptly dropped the R-squared to a modest 0.23. Still technically significant, sure, but it went from "soulmates" to "acquaintances who occasionally wave," which is roughly the demotion every raw hit deserves.

Wednesday paired imports of intellectual-property service charges with the durable-goods share of consumption, and it ran the identical playbook: shiny significant raw result, R-squared of 0.32, dazzling p-value -- followed by a detrended R-squared of 0.02 and a p-value of 0.44, i.e., the statistical equivalent of a shrug and a long silence. Textbook mirage. Thursday then gave us government imputed receipts versus discontinued U.S. official reserve assets, which had the decency to be boring in both versions -- nothing significant anywhere, a refreshing palate cleanser of pure noise. And Friday closed with the Producer Price Index for Computer Training Services against domestic finance company securities flows, which flopped in the raw regression but flirted with significance once detrended (p around 0.07, for the thrill-seekers). So another week in the books: one legitimately intriguing survivor, two gloriously debunked trend-illusions, and a comforting reminder that if you torture enough random data pairs, a few will eventually confess to anything. None of it means a thing, and that's the whole point.
