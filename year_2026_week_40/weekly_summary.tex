Welcome back, dear reader, to the only weekly newsletter that treats a random number generator and a federal economic database like a dating app. This week we swiped right four times and, as always, the results were a glorious monument to coincidence. Buckle up.

Monday served us the matchup of the century: Great Lakes wood product manufacturing versus mobile phone subscriptions across Europe and Central Asia. Because of course those two belong in the same regression. The raw version basically shrugged -- nothing significant, an R-squared you could trip over -- but then, plot twist, the detrended version actually coughed up a significant negative relationship. So after we strip out each series' own march through time, apparently fewer Great Lakes two-by-fours quietly coincide with more European cell phones, or vice versa. Is there a mechanism here? Absolutely not. Did it survive the detrending gauntlet? Weirdly, yes, which in this project is practically a standing ovation, even if what we're clapping for is almost certainly noise in a trenchcoat.

Tuesday brought the week's big flashy number: labor-force-nonparticipation among Black Americans against a discontinued measure of the wage share of national income, posting an R-squared of 0.89 in the raw regression. Point eight nine! Pop the champagne, call the Nobel committee, we've cracked macroeconomics! Except, as our heroic detrended check reminds us, two series that both drift majestically over decades will hold hands no matter what, and once we remove those trends the relationship deflates from "0.89 soulmates" to a much more modest 0.23. Still technically significant, sure, but this is the textbook trend-mirage -- the kind of raw hit that looks like destiny and is really just two lines going the same direction for eighty years. Wednesday did the same thing in reverse: intellectual-property import charges versus durable-goods consumption share looked impressively significant raw, then collapsed into total nothingness once detrended (p-value of 0.44, the statistical equivalent of a blank stare). Classic.

Thursday, mercifully, declined to participate in any drama whatsoever: government receipt imputations versus discontinued U.S. official reserve assets produced a p-value of 0.83 raw and an even more apathetic 0.53 detrended. Two series that looked at each other, felt absolutely nothing, and went home. Honestly refreshing. As ever, the standard disclaimer applies with great enthusiasm: these are randomly paired series, there is no theory here, no causal claim, and no reason to believe any of it. Spurious correlation isn't the risk in this project -- it's the entire business model. See you next week, when we'll once again mistake two upward-sloping lines for love.
