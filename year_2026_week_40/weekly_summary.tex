Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we feed a robot two completely unrelated economic time series, demand it find meaning, and then act shocked when it does. This week's contestants included Great Lakes wood product manufacturing, European cell phone subscriptions, discontinued labor force shares, intellectual property import fees, computer training price indexes, and -- a personal favorite -- the Import Price Index for Beverages and Tobacco. Nothing says rigorous science like regressing booze-and-cigarette prices against unfilled orders for camera parts. Buckle up.

Let's start with the week's trend-mirage hall of fame, Tuesday the 29th, where Depression-era wage-and-salary income shares were pitted against the number of Black Americans not in the labor force. The raw regression came back with an R-squared of 0.89 and a p-value with so many zeros after the decimal point it looks like a typo. Economists would weep. But then we detrend it, strip out the fact that both lines simply wander across the twentieth century, and the R-squared face-plants from 0.89 all the way down to 0.23. Still technically significant, sure, but mostly this is a textbook example of "two things drifting in the same era is not a relationship, it's a calendar." The computer-training price index versus finance company securities flows (the 2nd) pulled the same stunt in reverse: dead-on-arrival in the raw version, then suddenly perking up to a flirty p-value of 0.07 once detrended, which is the statistical equivalent of a result clearing its throat and saying "well, maybe."

Now, the one that actually deserves a raised eyebrow: Monday the 28th, wood product manufacturing in the Great Lakes versus European cell phone subscriptions. The raw regression was a nothingburger -- not significant, barely any explanatory power. But detrended, it got stronger, landing at an R-squared of 0.18 and a genuinely significant p-value of 0.02. That's the rare and precious case where a relationship shows up only after you remove the shared drift, meaning it survives the one test this whole project exists to administer. Do I have a theory for why Midwestern lumber output moves inversely with how many phones Europeans own? I do not. I will not be developing one. But it held up, so it gets the gold star.

The rest of the week was mercifully honest about its own pointlessness: Thursday's government imputations versus official reserve assets couldn't find a signal with a flashlight (R-squared of 0.0009, truly inspiring), and Saturday's tobacco-vs-cameras pairing looked significant raw then evaporated to a p-value of 0.76 once detrended, which is just the universe politely confirming nothing was there. As always, the standing reminder: these are randomly paired series, no causal claims are being made, and spurious correlation isn't the risk here -- it's the entire business model. See you next week for more statistically significant nonsense.
