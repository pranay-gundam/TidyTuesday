Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we grab two economic time series that have never met, never will, and never should, and force them into a linear regression like a reality-show producer who definitely knows what they're doing. As always, the reminder up top: these pairs are chosen at random, no causal story is intended, and if you find yourself nodding along to any of the ``relationships'' below, please go outside and touch a nonferrous metal.

Monday, October 19, kicked things off with a true humdinger: ``Weekday-Basis Seasonal Factors for Light Truck Production'' versus the ``Export Price Index for Nonferrous Metal Production.'' The raw regression produced an R-squared of roughly 0.00003, which is my favorite kind of number because it is, functionally, zero wearing a trench coat. A p-value of 0.93 confirmed that these two series regard each other with the warm indifference of strangers on a bus. The detrended version somehow managed to do even less. Two series, utterly unmoved by one another's existence. A palate cleanser. We stan a clean null.

Then came Tuesday, October 20, which tried to convince us that Latin American agricultural import prices are deeply entangled with the checkable deposits and currency held by the 50th-to-90th wealth percentiles (discontinued, naturally, because nothing says rigor like a dead series). The raw regression lit up with an R-squared of 0.27 and a p-value of 0.0006 -- oooh, significance, clutch the pearls! And then we detrended it, and the whole thing evaporated into an R-squared of 0.0008 and a p-value of 0.86. Textbook trend mirage. Two things drifted vaguely over time, the raw regression mistook ``both exist in chronological order'' for ``love,'' and detrending filed the annulment papers. This is precisely the kind of result this project was built to point at and laugh.

The real headliner, though, was Thursday, October 23: the DC minimum wage versus the Peoria, Illinois house price index. The raw regression came in hot with an R-squared of 0.77 -- genuinely the beefiest number of the week -- and a p-value with so many zeros it looked like it fell asleep on the keyboard. For one glorious moment I almost believed DC wage legislators were personally propping up Peoria real estate. And then, as is tradition, the detrended version strode in and knifed it: R-squared 0.0003, p-value 0.91. Both series just go up over decades, as money-flavored things tend to do, and once you strip out that shared upward march there's nothing left but noise shaking hands with noise. So three pairs, three nothingburgers, zero survivors in the detrended round -- which, honestly, is the most statistically honest week we could've hoped for. Draw no conclusions, trust no correlation, and I'll see you next week to do it all again.
