Welcome back to the weekly ritual where a computer picks two economic series out of a hat, mashes them together, and I pretend to be surprised by the results. This week we bravely paired the price of services with a discontinued overnight lending rate, Swiss central bank shenanigans from the 1970s with modern manufacturing order ratios, Asian air freight costs with restaurant job postings, and -- my personal favorite -- the average size of a medium-term bank loan with the growth rate of the entire national income. Truly the pairings of destiny.

Monday's contestant (August 10) came out swinging with a raw R-squared of 0.93, which for a hobby project is roughly the statistical equivalent of a slot machine paying out on the first pull. Chained services prices and a one-week interest rate marching in lockstep! Call the newspapers! Except, of course, both series were quietly drifting along over time, and the moment we stripped that drift out, the R-squared face-planted from 0.93 down to a limp 0.23. It's still technically "significant" in the detrended version, so I won't fully dunk on it, but let's be honest: that 0.93 was 99 percent shared trend and 1 percent actual acquaintance. Classic trend mirage, gone the instant you make it prove itself.

August 12 deserves a special mention for being the most dramatically useless result of the week: Swiss National Bank dollar purchases against the Deutsche Mark versus manufacturers' unfilled-orders ratios produced an R-squared of approximately 0.0000000000000003 and a coefficient of literally zero. The model was so confused it flagged a singular design matrix, which is math's polite way of saying "there is nothing here, please go home." The other two days were equally sleepy -- air freight and food-service job postings couldn't clear the significance bar in either the raw OR detrended version (p-values loitering around 0.19 and 0.11), and loan sizes versus real gross domestic income mustered an R-squared of 0.02 that collapsed to essentially nothing once detrended. Real income growth, it turns out, does not care about the size of your low-risk bank loan. Shocking.

As always, the mandatory reminder: these series were paired at random, and any relationship you see is almost certainly the universe's idea of a practical joke rather than evidence of anything. The whole point of this exercise is that spurious correlation is the house specialty -- I am not claiming Swiss currency interventions cause factory backlogs, nor that services inflation is secretly puppeteering discontinued interest rates. When even the one flashy 0.93 result crumbles the moment we ask it to work for a living, you know we're firmly in "for entertainment purposes only" territory. See you next week for more statistical nonsense.
