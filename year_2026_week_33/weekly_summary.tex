\section{Weekly Summary}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10,colframe=black!50,title=AI-Generated Content]
\noindent The summary below was written by Claude (Anthropic) from that week's regression outputs and series descriptions. It has not been reviewed or fact-checked by a human, and should be read as an automated, informal recap -- not analysis.
\end{tcolorbox}

Welcome back to the weekly parade of economic data being forced onto blind dates it never consented to. This week the machine paired up five sets of series that have absolutely no business being in the same room, ran regressions on them anyway, and produced results that will change nothing about your life. As always, none of this means anything, causally speaking, and I encourage you to keep repeating that to yourself like a soothing mantra as we proceed.

Monday, August 10, opened strong with the crossover event nobody asked for: the Chained CPI for Services versus the tragically discontinued 1-Week AMERIBOR interest rate. The raw regression came back with an R-squared of 0.93, which is the kind of number that makes a person want to draft a press release. Ninety-three percent! Practically destiny! Except, of course, both series spend their whole lives quietly drifting in the same direction over time, which is exactly the trend-mirage this entire project exists to mock. So we strip the trends out and — oh no — the R-squared faceplants down to 0.23. It's still technically "significant" at p equals 0.014, so I'll grudgingly admit this one didn't completely evaporate, which for our purposes is practically a miracle. Not a real relationship, mind you. Just a slightly less embarrassing coincidence than usual.

The rest of the week was a masterclass in nothing happening, and I say that with love. Tuesday's pairing of Swiss National Bank USD-against-DEM interventions with a manufacturers' unfilled-orders ratio produced a coefficient of literally zero, a singular design matrix, and enough NaNs to make the software quietly beg for retirement — turns out one of the series apparently sat perfectly flat, and you can't correlate your way out of a straight line. Wednesday matched Asian air freight import prices against food-service job postings on Indeed, which sounds thrilling and delivered a raw R-squared of 0.023 and a p-value of 0.19, i.e. absolutely nothing. Thursday put average bank loan size (also discontinued, this project is a graveyard) against Real Gross Domestic Income, and the detrended relationship confidently explained a heroic 0.3 percent of the variance. Bravo.

Friday closed us out with the share of "other loans" held by the top 0.1 percent versus — and I cannot stress how random this is — Czech consumers' feelings about their economic future. The raw p-value of 0.12 wasn't significant, the detrended p-value of 0.39 was even less significant, and somewhere the Czech Republic remains blissfully unaware it was conscripted into a regression about American billionaires' loan portfolios. So the final tally: one relationship that survived detrending well enough to raise a mildly interested eyebrow, and four that dissolved on contact with reality. Which is, frankly, a better hit rate than this project deserves. Reminder, as ever: these are randomly paired series, spurious correlation is the house special, and if you cite any of this in a real argument, that's between you and your conscience.
