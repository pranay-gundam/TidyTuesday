This week, in our ongoing quest to slam two completely unrelated economic series together and see what happens, the random number generator reached deep into the FRED barrel and pulled out a real odd couple: the Chained Consumer Price Index for services in U.S. cities, and -- I promise I am not making this up -- the 1-Week AMERIBOR Term Structure of Interest Rates, a series so beloved that it has since been DISCONTINUED. One of these measures how much your haircut and your streaming subscriptions cost, the other was a niche interest rate benchmark that quietly rode off into the sunset in late 2023. Naturally, we asked whether one explains the other. As one does.

And oh, what a raw regression it was. An R-squared of 0.93. Ninety-three percent! A p-value with fifteen zeros hiding behind the decimal point! If you glanced only at this, you might start drafting a Nobel acceptance speech about how services inflation is secretly governed by a defunct short-term lending rate. Truly the stuff of legend. Except, of course, both of these series were happily drifting along during an era of, you know, generally rising prices and interest rates, which is exactly the kind of shared-trend mirage this whole project exists to point at and cackle. Two lines going up together is not romance. It is just gravity for spreadsheets.

Enter the detrended version, the great destroyer of dreams. Once we strip out each series' own march-through-time, that glorious 0.93 collapses into a far more humble 0.23 R-squared. To be fair -- and I say this through gritted teeth -- the relationship does technically survive at a p-value of about 0.014, so it's not a total no-show like some of our trend-mirage disasters. There's a faint, wobbly little something left over after the trends are gone, a positive nudge that refuses to fully die. I would call it "intriguing" if I had any self-respect left, which after several weeks of this I do not.

But let us all take a deep, grounding breath and remember what is actually happening here: a computer picked two series out of a hat, one of which no longer exists, and we ran a straight line through them for entertainment. There is no theory, no causal story, no reason on Earth that consumer service prices should answer to a retired interest rate benchmark. Spurious correlation is the house specialty, not the exception, and a survivable-but-shrunken detrended p-value is about the most honest outcome this silly machine can produce. Enjoy the 0.014 responsibly, and please do not put it in a financial model. See you next week for more statistically meaningless companionship.
