\section{Weekly Summary}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10,colframe=black!50,title=AI-Generated Content]
\noindent The summary below was written by Claude (Anthropic) from that week's regression outputs and series descriptions. It has not been reviewed or fact-checked by a human, and should be read as an automated, informal recap -- not analysis.
\end{tcolorbox}

Welcome back to another thrilling week of pointing a random-number cannon at the Federal Reserve's database and screaming "correlate, you cowards!" As always, the machine dutifully pulled five pairs of economic series that have no business being in the same room together, ran a regression on each, and then ran it again after stripping out the shared upward-and-to-the-right drift that makes everything look meaningful. Spoiler: it mostly did not survive that second step. It rarely does. That is the whole point.

Monday's headliner was Coinbase Bitcoin versus the San Francisco Tech Pulse index, which posted a raw R-squared of 0.70, a number so robust I briefly considered quitting my job to become a quant. Then the detrending happened and the R-squared crumpled to a limp 0.14 -- still technically "significant," but in the way a gas station hot dog is technically food. Two things that both went up over the years shocking absolutely no one by appearing to move together. File under: trend mirage, gently deflated, mostly a nothingburger with a faint whiff of drama.

Tuesday and Wednesday were the palate cleansers. Philadelphia's capital expenditures diffusion index versus some real user cost of money index turned in a p-value of 0.89 raw and 0.94 detrended, which is statistics-speak for "these two have never met and would like to keep it that way." Then Thursday gave us Italian foreign-exchange interventions from the 1990s versus real-estate loan delinquency rates: a coquettish little raw p-value of 0.04 that vanished into a shrug of 0.35 the moment the trend was removed. And Friday's recession-indicator-versus-current-account pairing didn't even bother pretending, posting an R-squared of 0.005 and a p-value of 0.60 that felt almost aggressive in its refusal to cooperate. I respect the honesty.

But wait -- Thursday, er, the last one, actually earned a raised eyebrow. U.S. exports to newly industrialized countries versus the state-and-local government's share of GDP came in significant raw (R-squared 0.42) and, more interestingly, stayed significant after detrending, even flipping sign to a negative relationship in the process. That's the rare specimen this whole silly project pretends to be hunting for: a link that doesn't evaporate the second you remove the shared drift. Now, before anyone drafts a working paper, let me be crystal clear: these are randomly paired series, there is no theory here, no causal story, and the responsible interpretation of "exports go up, government spending share goes down" is a firm and loving "who knows, probably nothing." Spurious correlation is the house special, not the exception -- I just occasionally serve one that's been aged a little longer. See you next week for more numbers that mean nothing with great confidence.
