Welcome back to the segment where we heroically pair up economic time series that have never met, force them into a regression, and act shocked at the results. This week the random number generator was feeling especially whimsical, so buckle up.

Monday gave us the crown jewel: Coinbase Bitcoin versus the San Francisco Tech Pulse, a tech index so vibrant it was discontinued in 2020. The raw regression came in with an R-squared of about 0.70, which in this project counts as basically a spiritual experience -- seventy percent! A p-value with seventeen zeros! For a moment I genuinely thought we'd cracked the market. And then, as always, we ran the detrended version, where the shared "everything went up over time" story gets stripped out, and the R-squared face-planted from 0.70 all the way down to 0.14. So yes, Bitcoin and a defunct tech index both trended upward for years. Groundbreaking. This is the exact trend-mirage the project loves to point at and laugh: two lines going up together is not romance, it's just gravity in reverse. To its modest credit, the detrended version was still technically "significant," but at that R-squared I wouldn't invite it to dinner.

Tuesday was the palate cleanser of pure nothing: Philadelphia's Current Physical Plant Capital Expenditures diffusion index against the Real User Cost Index of MSI-M2M, a name I am contractually obligated to pretend I understand. The raw regression posted an R-squared of 0.0006 and a p-value of 0.89, which is statistics-speak for "these two have never been in the same room." The detrended version somehow did even less, if that's possible. No trend mirage, no relationship, no drama -- just two series shrugging at each other across the void. Beautiful, honestly. This is what most days actually look like.

Wednesday brought Banca d'Italia's foreign exchange interventions from the '90s versus the delinquency rate on real estate loans at the 100 largest banks, because of course it did. The raw regression squeaked in "significant" at a p-value of 0.04 with a whopping 9.5 percent R-squared, the kind of result that gets you a participation trophy. Then we detrended it and the p-value ballooned to 0.35, meaning the relationship evaporated the instant we took away its trendy little crutch. Another mirage identified and dismissed. As always, please remember that everything here is randomly paired series with zero causal claim and even less statistical rigor -- spurious correlation isn't the bug, it's the entire personality of this project. See you next week, when the dice roll again.
