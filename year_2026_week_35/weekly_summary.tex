Welcome back to the weekly parade of economic non sequiturs, where a computer flings two innocent time series into a blender and we all pretend to be surprised by the smoothie. This week the machine got creative, and by "creative" I mean it paired up things that have never once been in the same room. Buckle up.

Monday kicked us off by wedding "Other Business Receivables Owned by Finance Companies" to a nonfarm employment diffusion index, and the relationship was so weak it barely qualified as an acquaintance. R-squared of 0.006, p-value hovering around 0.10, and detrending did absolutely nothing to rescue it. Two series that looked at each other, shrugged, and walked away. Tuesday somehow did even less: pairing auto production seasonal factors with Romanian reissue patents produced a glorious wall of NaNs, which is the statistical equivalent of the model filing for divorce before the wedding. Nothing to see here, which is honestly on brand.

Then Wednesday and Thursday actually made me sit up. The San Francisco Tech Pulse versus the labor force of women with master's degrees showed a raw relationship that, remarkably, got STRONGER after detrending (R-squared climbing from 0.10 to 0.14, p-value diving into the billionths) -- so that one isn't just two things drifting upward together, there's some genuine wiggle-matching going on. And Thursday was the showstopper: an Employment Cost Index for manufacturing professionals against the price of groceries, sporting a raw R-squared of 0.95 that will make any layperson gasp. Cooler heads note it deflates to 0.43 once you strip the trends -- so most of that jaw-dropper was just two things marching upward through time like everything else in a modern economy -- but 0.43 with a laughably tiny p-value still holds up as a real link. Wages and food prices moving together? Groundbreaking. Someone alert an economist, they'll be so relieved to hear that inflation is, in fact, inflation.

Friday closed us out gently, with credit intermediation prices and department store sales sharing all the chemistry of two strangers in an elevator: nothing significant, raw or detrended, and department stores continuing their proud tradition of not correlating with anything, including their own survival. As always, the mandatory reminder: these pairings are drawn at random, none of this implies causation, and the whole point of the exercise is to watch spurious correlations strut around in a lab coat. When something like the grocery-wage link survives detrending, we raise an eyebrow; when it doesn't, we point and laugh. This week gave us both, which is about as good as random noise gets. See you next week for more.
