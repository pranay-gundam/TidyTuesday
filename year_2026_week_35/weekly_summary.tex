\section{Weekly Summary}

\noindent The summary below was written by Claude (Anthropic) from that week's regression outputs and series descriptions. It has not been reviewed or fact-checked by a human, and should be read as an automated, informal recap -- not analysis.

Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we shove two economic series that have never met into a blender and pretend the resulting smoothie means something. As always, the machine picks these pairings at random, no theory or dignity involved, and any correlation you see should be treated with roughly the same scientific respect you'd give a fortune cookie. With that ceremonial disclaimer out of the way, let's tour the wreckage.

Monday kicked things off with Other Business Receivables Owned by Finance Companies staring longingly at a Total Nonfarm Diffusion Index, and the two of them could barely be bothered to acknowledge each other. An R-squared of 0.006 -- yes, that's roughly six-tenths of one percent of anything explained -- and a p-value loitering around 0.10 in both the raw and detrended versions. The rare case where nothing changes when you remove the trend, purely because there was no relationship to remove in the first place. Truly a nothingburger, served cold. Tuesday somehow topped it: Auto Production Seasonal Factors versus Reissue Patents Originating in Romania produced a big beautiful pile of NaNs, the statistical equivalent of the regression getting up and walking out of the room. I don't blame it. Pairing a monthly seasonal-adjustment fudge factor with annual Romanian patent reissues is exactly the kind of matchup this project was built to humiliate, and it responded by refusing to compute at all.

Wednesday is where it gets mildly interesting, if you squint. The (discontinued, RIP) San Francisco Tech Pulse index went up against the number of women aged 25-64 with master's degrees in the labor force, and the raw regression coughed up a p-value with seven zeros after the decimal -- extremely "significant" looking. Normally this is the exact moment I put on my dunking shoes and wait for the detrended version to expose it as two lines that both drifted upward over time holding hands for no reason. Except... it didn't fully collapse. Detrending actually nudged the R-squared up from 0.10 to 0.14 and the relationship stayed rock-solidly significant. So credit where it's due: something wiggles together here even after stripping the shared drift. Do I have a mechanism connecting tech-sector vibes to the credentialing of professional women? No. Am I going to invent one? Also no. But it's a genuinely less-fake result than usual, which around here counts as a national holiday.

Then Thursday came in swinging with the raw regression of the week: the Employment Cost Index for manufacturing professionals versus the Consumer Price Index for Food at Home, posting a jaw-dropping R-squared of 0.95. Ninety-five percent! Break out the champagne, we've discovered that... wait for it... two price-and-cost indexes both went up over the decades. Shocking. This is the textbook trend mirage, and sure enough, once you detrend, the R-squared face-plants down to 0.43. Now, that's not nothing -- it does stay significant, so there's some real co-movement in the wiggles once you subtract the "everything gets more expensive over time" backdrop -- but that 0.95 was doing a lot of theatrical heavy lifting, and I refuse to let it take a bow. In summary: two total whiffs, one suspicious survivor, and one drama queen that lost half its glory the second we made it work for a living. Same time next week, same complete absence of causal claims.
