Welcome back to the only newsletter where a computer flings two unrelated economic time series into a blender every morning and we all pretend to be surprised by what comes out. This week the machine really outdid itself, serving up a lineup of pairings so arbitrary that even the spreadsheet seemed embarrassed. As always, the standing disclaimer: nothing here is a real finding, no series is causing any other series, and if you take investment advice from this document that is between you and your therapist.

Monday, August 24, we matched Other Business Receivables Owned by Finance Companies against a nonfarm employment diffusion index, because why not. The raw regression produced an R-squared of roughly 0.007, which is a fancy way of saying the line explains basically nothing, and a p-value of about 0.10 that couldn't quite muster the enthusiasm to call itself significant. Then we detrended it, and -- hold onto your hats -- absolutely nothing changed. Same nothing, now trend-free. A rare case where the raw and detrended versions agree completely, and what they agree on is that these two series have never met and would not recognize each other at a party.

Tuesday, August 25, was the highlight of the week for connoisseurs of technical failure. We paired Auto Production Seasonal Factors (monthly) with Reissue Patents Originating in Romania (annual, and last seen in 2020), and the regression returned a glorious wall of zeros and NaNs. That's the statistics equivalent of the machine staring at us in silence. Turns out asking a monthly seasonal fudge-factor to explain the number of Romanian reissue patents produces exactly the intellectual content you'd expect: none, formatted as errors. Chef's kiss. Truly the correlation we deserved.

Then Wednesday, August 26, the week suddenly grew a spine. We ran the San Francisco Tech Pulse against the labor force of women with master's degrees, and the raw regression came in significant with an R-squared near 0.10. Fine, you say, both drift upward over time, classic trend mirage, dunk incoming. Except this one refused to collapse -- detrended, it got stronger, R-squared climbing to about 0.14 with a p-value in the "three zeros then a three" range. So after we stripped out the shared upward drift, a relationship was still standing there, waving. One could spin a tale about a booming tech economy pulling more highly educated women into the workforce, and it would even sound plausible, which is precisely why I'm legally obligated to remind you it's still two randomly grabbed series and proves nothing. But credit where due: in a week that also produced a page of NaNs, this was a genuinely respectable little result. See you next week for more statistical noise dressed up as insight.
