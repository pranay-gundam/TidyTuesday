Welcome back to the segment where we bravely fling unrelated economic data at each other and pretend to be surprised by what sticks. This week the random number gods really outdid themselves, opening on Monday with a matchup between the "Discussion About Pandemics Index for Lithuania" and U.S. federal borrowing from the public. Two things that have absolutely nothing to do with each other, and yet — brace yourselves — the raw regression came back significant, and it actually survived detrending too. So even after we strip out the shared drift, Lithuanian pandemic chatter and American public debt still hold hands a little. Is it real? Almost certainly not. Am I contractually obligated to note that this is the "rarer, more interesting case"? Yes, and I hate that.

Tuesday paired U.S. coal CO2 emissions with a medical price index for congenital anomalies, and honestly this one is the overachiever of the week. The raw regression explained about 61 percent of the variation, and instead of collapsing when detrended, it held on at a robust 56 percent with the slope actually getting steeper. I want to be clear that there is no universe in which coal smoke is setting the price of specialized medical services, but the numbers are sitting here looking me dead in the eye like they mean it. Wednesday, meanwhile, was pure comedy: gross federal debt versus business equipment leases owned by finance companies produced a raw relationship with a laughably tiny R-squared, and detrending somehow made it "more significant" while explaining almost nothing. Truly the statistical equivalent of confidently walking into the wrong room.

Thursday gave us the week's designated punching bag, and I love it dearly. Construction quits versus the work-from-home rate for women started off with an R-squared of essentially zero and a p-value of 0.86 — the raw regression basically shrugged and left. Then we detrend, and suddenly it lurches to life with a chunky, very significant relationship out of nowhere. This is a beautiful reminder that "significant after detrending" is evidence, not proof, and that random noise is perfectly capable of getting dressed up for the occasion. Friday's national wages versus manufacturing employment in Ogden, Utah was the classic trend mirage: an eye-watering 80 percent R-squared in the raw version that deflated to a much humbler 40 percent once the shared upward march of time was removed. Impressive headline, ordinary story.

We closed Saturday with Midwest shelter prices versus the price of a whole chicken in the West, which posted the flashiest raw number of the entire week — 94 percent R-squared, the kind of figure that makes people quit their jobs to write a book. And then detrending did what detrending does, dragging it down to a merely-fine 37 percent. Both things go up over time because most prices go up over time; shocking, I know. As always, the mandatory disclaimer: these are randomly paired series with zero causal claim intended, and spurious correlation is the whole point of the exercise, not an unfortunate accident. If you took investment advice from a chicken and a shelter index this week, that is between you and your conscience.
