Welcome back to another thrilling installment of Numbers That Have No Business Being Near Each Other. This week the random-pair generator really outdid itself, kicking off Monday by asking whether Lithuania's "Discussion About Pandemics Index" -- yes, that is a real series, and yes, its popularity score is a heartbreaking 1 -- could predict how much the U.S. government borrows from the public. The raw regression said "sure, a little," with an R-squared around 0.20, and here is the genuinely surprising twist: when we strip out the shared drift, the relationship barely flinches. It holds up in the detrended version too. Do I believe that Lithuanian pandemic chatter governs American public borrowing? No. Absolutely not. But it survived the one test I built specifically to embarrass it, so I am obligated to raise a single eyebrow.

Tuesday paired U.S. coal CO2 emissions with a medical price index for congenital anomalies, because of course it did, and this was the star of the week. The raw regression posted a whopping 0.61 R-squared, and instead of collapsing like a folding chair once detrended, it actually stayed strong at 0.56 -- and the coefficient roughly doubled, which is the statistical equivalent of a series flexing at you. I have no earthly theory here beyond "two things happened over the same twenty years," but as trend-mirages go, this one refuses to evaporate, which puts it in the rare and unsettling "actually kind of interesting" bin. Please do not build policy on coal-fueled anomaly pricing.

Then came the classic dunk we all know and love. Wednesday matched the Market Value of Gross Federal Debt against business equipment leases, and the raw regression breathlessly reported a screamingly significant p-value with a laughable R-squared of 0.09 -- lots of confidence, almost no explanatory power, the dream. Amusingly the detrended version was slightly stronger, so even this one didn't fully faceplant. Thursday, meanwhile, delivered the week's most beautiful faceplant: construction quits versus women's work-from-home rate had an R-squared of basically 0.0005 raw, a p-value of 0.86, the numeric shrug of the century -- and then detrending resurrected it into a significant 0.30 relationship out of nowhere. Truly a series that means nothing until you make it mean something. Friday capped things off with national wages versus manufacturing employment in Ogden, Utah, which posted a gaudy 0.80 raw R-squared that promptly deflated to 0.40 once the shared upward march of time was removed -- respectable, but exactly the kind of half-mirage this project exists to poke at.

As always, the mandatory reminder: these are randomly paired economic series, glued together by a script with no supervision and even less shame. Nothing here implies causation, correlation-with-meaning, or anything a responsible person would cite. Spurious is the baseline expectation, not the exception, and the only reason we detrend at all is so I can tell you which coincidences at least tried a little harder than the others. See you next week for more numbers pretending to know each other.
