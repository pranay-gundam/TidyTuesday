Welcome back to another thrilling week of shoving unrelated economic data into a blender and pressing "regress." As always, we are drawing zero real conclusions from any of this, because the whole enterprise is a monument to statistical mischief. But my goodness, did the numbers put on a show.

Monday's pairing was a real work of art: New England commercial electric power prices versus a national price index for "Symptoms, Signs, and Ill-Defined Conditions" medical spending. Yes, that is a real series, and no, I did not make up that name to sound spooky. The raw regression came roaring out of the gate with an R-squared of 0.93, which is the kind of number that makes a person want to call a press conference and announce that New England electricity secretly governs the cost of America's vaguest ailments. And then we detrended it, and the whole thing evaporated into thin air -- R-squared of 0.02, a p-value of basically 0.51, a coefficient so close to zero it needs a magnifying glass. Textbook trend mirage. Two series wandered upward over the decades holding hands, we assumed romance, and detrending revealed they were simply two strangers escalating the same staircase. Absolutely nothing to see here, which is exactly what we love to see.

Tuesday, however, got weird in the fun way. We matched the active real estate listing count in Flathead County, Montana -- a specific and delightful place -- against U.S. imports of maintenance and repair services. The raw regression was actually the weaker of the two here (R-squared a humble 0.12), and then, in a plot twist worthy of a dramatic gasp, the detrended version got stronger, climbing to 0.19 with a p-value hovering around four in ten million. That means once you strip out the long-run drift from both, there's still a wiggle-for-wiggle relationship left standing. In this project, a detrended survivor is the rarest of beasts, so allow me to over-emote appropriately: incredible, unprecedented, someone alert Montana. My extremely scientific theory is that when houses pile up on the market in Flathead County, the nation collectively decides it's time to fix things, and imported repair services surge. Is that true? Almost certainly not. Is it a fun sentence? Yes.

So this week gave us one perfect fake and one suspiciously durable coincidence, which is a fantastic ratio for a project whose entire premise is that spurious correlation is the norm and real relationships are the exception. Please do not refinance anything, relocate to Flathead County, or short New England electricity based on these results. We are two random series and a linear model out here, having a laugh. See you next week for more numbers doing things they were never asked to do.
