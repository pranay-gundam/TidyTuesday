Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we throw two unrelated economic time series into a blender and act shocked at whatever purée comes out. This week the algorithm, in its infinite wisdom, decided to introduce New England electricity prices to a medical index for "symptoms, signs, and ill-defined conditions," which is genuinely the most poetic thing this project has produced. Nothing says rigorous science like pairing the cost of keeping the lights on with a category literally named after doctors shrugging.

Monday's electricity-versus-vaguely-defined-ailments matchup came out swinging with an R-squared of 0.93 in the raw regression, a number so high it could make you briefly believe you'd discovered something. And then, right on cue, we detrended it, and the R-squared face-planted to a heroic 0.02 with a p-value of 0.51. Translation: the only thing these two series had in common was that both of them went up over time, like nearly everything measured in dollars since 1971. This is the textbook trend mirage, the bread and butter of this entire operation, and I regret to inform you it was never real. Pour one out.

Tuesday paired the active housing listing count in Flathead County, Montana with U.S. imports of maintenance and repair services, and honestly this one is more interesting precisely because it's less flashy. The raw regression was weak (R-squared of 0.12), but instead of dying when detrended it actually got stronger, climbing to 0.19 with a p-value with more zeros than a lottery ticket. It's still not a strong relationship by any human standard, but the fact that stripping out the shared drift made it more significant rather than less is the rare, mildly respectable outcome we're contractually obligated to point at and go "huh." Do Montana home listings secretly govern the nation's appetite for imported repairs? No. But it survived the detrend, so it gets a participation trophy.

The real standout was Wednesday, where wholesale trade payrolls met the Dover, Delaware house price index and refused to let go. The raw regression posted an R-squared of 0.73, and instead of collapsing under detrending it held firm at 0.73 with an absurdly tiny p-value. That's the good stuff, the kind of relationship that persists even after we take away its trend-shaped crutches, and if I were a less responsible person I'd write a think-piece about how aggregate payrolls drive Delaware home values. I am, however, legally and morally required to remind you that these are randomly paired series with zero causal claim attached; even a relationship that survives detrending is just two numbers wiggling in agreement, not a law of nature. Spurious correlation is the house specialty here, so enjoy the pretty numbers responsibly and please do not trade on Delaware real estate because of a hobby PDF.
