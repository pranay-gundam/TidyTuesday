This week was a real barn-burner, if by "barn-burner" you mean we ran the regression machine exactly once and then presumably went to lie down. On the docket for September 21st: the Producer Price Index for Commercial Electric Power Distribution in New England, valiantly paired with a Medical Services Price Index for "Symptoms, Signs, and Ill-Defined Conditions." Yes, that is a real category. Yes, I too find it soothing that somewhere in the federal statistical apparatus there is an official price index for feeling vaguely unwell.

And oh, what a raw regression it was. R-squared of 0.93. A p-value with so many zeros after the decimal point it looks like a typo. If you squinted at nothing but this table, you'd walk away convinced that the cost of powering New England businesses is basically dictated by how much it costs to treat undiagnosable ailments -- a bold theory that would revolutionize both cardiology and the electric grid. Someone alert the Nobel committee. Hold their calls, actually.

Then, as it so often does, the detrended regression walked into the room and turned on the lights. Strip out the fact that both of these numbers have simply drifted upward for decades like everything else in a modern economy, and the mighty 0.93 collapses to a heartbreaking 0.02, with a p-value of 0.51 -- which in technical terms means "we have learned absolutely nothing." The relationship wasn't a relationship at all; it was two lines going up together because that's what prices do. A textbook trend mirage, and honestly a beautiful one. I got emotionally invested and everything.

As always, the fine print: these series are paired at random, there is no theory here, and no one should be reorganizing their portfolio around the deep spiritual bond between New England kilowatts and ill-defined medical conditions. Spurious is the house style, not the exception. Tune in next week, when we will once again mistake gravity for insight and act deeply surprised about it.
