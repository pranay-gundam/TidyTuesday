\section{Weekly Summary}

Welcome back to the corner of the internet where a computer flings two unrelated economic time series at each other every morning and I pretend to be surprised by what happens. This week the machine really committed to the bit, and I'm delighted to report a healthy mix of embarrassing trend-mirages, one or two suspiciously durable relationships, and one number so large it should probably be reported to the authorities.

We opened Sunday with New England commercial electric power prices squaring off against a medical price index for, and I quote, "Symptoms; Signs; and Ill-Defined Conditions." Frankly a mood. The raw regression came out looking like a soulmate connection, R-squared of 0.93, a p-value with more zeros than my checking account. Then we detrended it and the whole romance evaporated on contact: R-squared cratered to 0.02 and the p-value strolled up to 0.51, i.e. a resounding "who?" This is the textbook trend mirage this project was born to mock. Two things went up over time. That's the entire story. Ill-defined conditions indeed. Monday's matchup was almost the inverse and much more respectable: active home listings in Flathead County, Montana versus U.S. imports of maintenance and repair services. The raw relationship was actually kind of weak (R-squared 0.12), but the detrended version got stronger, not weaker, holding onto a tiny but genuinely significant link. Not a blockbuster, but the rare case where stripping out the trend made the thing look better. I have no theory. Montana listings and global repair-service imports. Sure.

Tuesday is where things got uncomfortably real. Wholesale trade payrolls versus the house price index for Dover, Delaware posted a strong raw relationship (R-squared 0.73) that then refused to die when detrended, staying at 0.73 with a p-value roughly the size of a Planck length. That's the good stuff, the actually-worth-a-raised-eyebrow result, though I'll gently remind you that "aggregate wholesale payrolls tend to move with the economy" and "Dover house prices tend to move with the economy" is not exactly a Nobel-worthy causal chain. Wednesday brought a Southwest central-bank-services GDP figure against a gloriously specific discontinued banking metric, which was borderline in the raw run (p-value flirting at 0.05) but firmed up into something significant and negative once detrended. Modest, but it survived, so I'll allow it.

Thursday gave us the education-and-communication CPI versus New Mexico's tax exemptions under age 65, and this one actually held up too: R-squared climbed from 0.32 raw to 0.51 detrended, both wildly significant. I refuse to explain why the cost of communication tracks New Mexican tax exemptions, and you should refuse to believe it means anything. Which brings us to Friday, the grand finale, in which "shares of gross domestic income" was pitted against a discontinued Peabody, Massachusetts house price index, and the model briefly gained sentience: the detrended coefficient came back as 57 trillion, with a p-value of 0.82, which is math's polite way of saying the design matrix was basically singular and the whole thing is nonsense. A coefficient in the tens of trillions and no significance whatsoever is the statistical equivalent of shouting confidently in an empty room.

As always, the standing disclaimer: these are randomly paired series, glued together for entertainment, with zero causal intent and no real statistical rigor behind the drama. Spurious correlation isn't the bug here, it's the entire product. A couple of this week's results held up through detrending, which is genuinely more than we usually get, but "held up in my silly little regression" and "means something about the world" are separated by a canyon roughly 57 trillion units wide. See you next week.
