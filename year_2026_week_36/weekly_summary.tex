Welcome back to the only weekly ritual where a computer shoves two unrelated economic series into a blender and we all lean in to see what color the smoothie turns out. This week the machine really outdid itself in the department of "series nobody has thought about since they were discontinued." We had a house price index for Ft. Lauderdale trying to hold hands with a defunct SBA small-business loan metric, durable-goods weekly hours flirting with foreign official demand deposits, U.S. Virgin Islands imports of non-manufactured commodities gazing longingly at Dutch business confidence, and, in the grand finale, grocery wholesaler inventories versus real GDP of social assistance in the Southeast. Truly the crossover episodes we did not ask for.

Monday's Fort Lauderdale housing versus zombie loan series was a nothingburger in both directions -- p-value of 0.33 raw, 0.18 detrended, R-squareds hovering around "please stop looking at me." No trend mirage, no real link, just two series politely ignoring each other. Tuesday's durable-goods hours versus foreign deposits was more theatrical: the raw regression flashed a "significant" p-value of 0.006 and I nearly printed a press release, but the moment we stripped out each series' own time trend the relationship evaporated into a p-value of 0.82 and an R-squared of basically zero. That, friends, is the textbook trend mirage this project exists to mock: two things drifting through the decades in vaguely coordinated fashion, mistaken for romance.

Then things got weird in a good way. Wednesday paired Virgin Islands imports with Netherlands services confidence, and here the raw version shrugged (p-value 0.59) while the detrended version actually perked up to a significant 0.014. It's not a strong relationship -- the R-squared is a humble 0.03, meaning it explains roughly the amount of variance you'd find under a couch cushion -- but it survived detrending, which technically makes it the more "respectable" finding of the week. And Friday's grocery inventories versus Southeast social-assistance GDP was the actual standout: significant in the detrended version (p-value 0.037) with an R-squared of 0.16, our biggest number all week, and even a nice negative slope for dramatic flavor. Somewhere a theorist is ready to explain how grocery warehouse stockpiles secretly foretell social assistance spending in the American Southeast. That theorist should lie down.

Because -- and I cannot stress this enough -- these pairs are chosen at random, with all the scientific rigor of pulling refrigerator magnets out of a hat. There is no causal story here, no hypothesis, no rigorous claim, and anything that looks "significant" is one entry in a long parade where spurious correlation is the house special, not the surprise. So enjoy the grocery-inventory-versus-welfare-GDP mystery for exactly what it is: a fun coincidence that held up marginally better than the rest, in a project whose entire premise is that holding up marginally better is still, statistically speaking, meaningless. See you next week for more.
