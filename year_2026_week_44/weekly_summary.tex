Welcome back to the weekly parade of economic data that has absolutely no business being in the same room together, let alone the same scatterplot. This week our little random-number goblin reached into the FRED barrel five times and pulled out such crowd-pleasers as a Danish alternative-energy stock index, Chilean transactions in U.S. agency bonds, the childhood poverty rolls of Leon County, Florida, and -- because why not -- high-tier San Francisco home prices. As always, these pairings are chosen by a machine with no taste, no agenda, and no understanding of causality, so please do not go rewiring your worldview based on anything below.

Monday kicked us off with Denmark's clean-energy index versus a long-discontinued Polish recession indicator, and I am thrilled to report it actually survived the detrending guillotine. The raw regression found a relationship, the detrended version found an even stronger one, and the R-squared politely crept up from a dizzying six percent to a staggering seven. Be still my heart. Is it a real link? Almost certainly not -- the Polish recession flag stopped reporting in 2022 and the whole thing explains roughly nothing -- but by the brutal standards of this project, "holds up after detrending" is basically a Nobel nomination, so I am legally obligated to call it out as the rare interesting one. Tuesday then brought us crashing back to Earth: motor-vehicle electrical equipment export prices against Chilean bond transactions produced a p-value of 0.43, which is statistics-speak for "we found literally nothing and we're proud of it." Zero for the detrended version too. A perfect, honest void.

Wednesday is where the trend-mirage museum opens its doors. Consumer motor-vehicle leases versus foreign mortgage-backed securities posted a jaw-dropping raw R-squared of 0.81 -- eighty-one percent! -- the kind of number that would make a lazy analyst start drafting a theory. Then we detrended it, and it sagged to 0.44, which, I'll admit, is still suspiciously alive and clinging on. So file that one under "surprisingly resilient but probably still two things that both wandered around in the 2010s together." Thursday's covered employment versus Midwest house prices pulled the exact same stunt: a gorgeous raw R-squared of 0.79 that deflated to a sad 0.17 once we stripped out the shared march of time. Classic. Everything grows, therefore everything correlates, therefore nothing means anything. It's the circle of life, GDP edition.

Friday closed us out with the week's most honest self-destruction: Leon County child poverty versus fancy San Francisco home prices looked respectable in the raw regression (R-squared 0.42, a confident little p-value), and then detrending absolutely vaporized it -- p-value of 0.71, R-squared of basically zero, the coefficient flipping sign out of sheer embarrassment. A textbook trend mirage, dunked on exactly as the gods of this project intended. So the weekly tally: one plucky survivor, two that limped through with a fraction of their swagger, and two that evaporated the moment we asked them to prove themselves. Which is, frankly, a better hit rate than usual for a hobby that pairs economic time series by throwing darts blindfolded. Remember, folks: none of this is a claim about anything, spurious correlation is the house specialty, and we will do it all again next week with equal rigor and zero shame.
