\section{Weekly Summary}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10,colframe=black!50,title=AI-Generated Content]
\noindent The summary below was written by Claude (Anthropic) from that week's regression outputs and series descriptions. It has not been reviewed or fact-checked by a human, and should be read as an automated, informal recap -- not analysis.
\end{tcolorbox}

Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we throw two unrelated economic series into a blender and solemnly report whatever comes out. As always, let me get the fine print out of the way before anyone frames a plot and sends it to their financial advisor: these pairs are chosen at random, nobody is claiming anyone causes anyone, and the house specialty here is the spurious correlation. If you came for rigor, you have taken a wrong turn. If you came to watch a Danish green-energy index get regressed against a discontinued Polish recession dummy, pull up a chair.

Monday did exactly that, pairing the OMX Copenhagen Alternative Energy Price Index with a Polish recession indicator that has been dead since 2022. The raw regression found a "significant" relationship, which sounds thrilling until you notice the R-squared was a crushing 0.06, meaning we explained roughly six percent of anything. The real plot twist: the detrended version actually got slightly stronger (a whole 0.07! call the Nobel committee). So technically this one survives detrending, which in this project counts as the rarest of beasts, except the effect is so microscopic it is less a "genuine link" and more two series politely nodding at each other across a crowded room. Tuesday, mercifully, delivered the week's one honest result: car-part export prices versus Chilean purchases of U.S. agency bonds produced absolutely nothing, raw or detrended, p-values a proud 0.43 and 0.90. Nothing. Nada. A pair so uncorrelated it restores my faith in the universe.

Then came Wednesday, the drama queen of the week. Consumer motor vehicle leases owned by finance companies versus foreign-held mortgage-backed securities posted a jaw-dropping raw R-squared of 0.81. Eighty-one percent! I nearly stood up and applauded. And then, as is tradition, the detrending guillotine came down -- and while the relationship did survive (R-squared 0.44, still significant), it got cut nearly in half, which is the statistical equivalent of a souffle deflating the moment you open the oven. A big chunk of that glorious 0.81 was just two financial series drifting through the decades together, as financial series do. Still, holding onto 0.44 after detrending is genuinely more interesting than most weeks manage, so I will grudgingly tip my hat.

Thursday rounded things out with Covered Employment versus a Midwest house price index, and this is the trend-mirage this whole project was built to mock: a magnificent raw R-squared of 0.79 that collapses to 0.17 once you strip out the shared "everything goes up over time" drift. Employment rises, house prices rise, and a linear regression breathlessly concludes they are soulmates. They are not. They just both happened to grow for fifty years, like most things denominated in dollars or people. So to recap: one pair of true strangers, two trend-fueled romances that cooled considerably under scrutiny, and one tiny Danish-Polish flirtation that technically held up while meaning essentially nothing. Another week, another reminder that correlation, causation, and coincidence are three very different things, and this newsletter proudly traffics in only the third.
