Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we throw two unrelated economic series into a blender and act surprised by whatever sludge comes out. This week the dartboard served up some truly inspired matchups, and I am thrilled to report that at least one of them survived contact with reality. Only one. Expectations: appropriately subterranean.

Monday kicked off with the OMX Copenhagen Alternative Energy Price Index facing down a discontinued OECD recession indicator for Poland, because nothing says "rigorous science" like regressing Danish solar stocks against a binary flag for Polish recessions that stopped updating in 2022. The raw regression coughed up a p-value with forty-some zeroes after the decimal point, which sounds impressive until you notice the R-squared is a whopping six percent. And here is the genuinely surprising part: the detrended version actually got slightly stronger instead of collapsing. Seven percent R-squared, still absurdly "significant." Now, before anyone drafts a thesis titled "Copenhagen Clean Energy as a Leading Indicator of Polish Economic Doom," let me remind you that explaining seven percent of anything is the statistical equivalent of showing up to a marathon and jogging to the mailbox. It holds up better than the usual trend mirage, sure, but "better than garbage" is not "good."

Tuesday mercifully restored order to the universe. We paired a motor vehicle electronics export price index against foreign net transactions of U.S. long-term agency bonds by Chile -- a crossover event nobody asked for -- and the result was a magnificent, resounding nothing. Raw p-value of 0.43, detrended p-value of 0.90, an R-squared so small it rounds to the number of friends this project has made. This is the correlation machine working exactly as intended: two random series, zero relationship, no drama. Chef's kiss.

Then Wednesday had to go and get interesting. Consumer motor vehicle leases owned by finance companies versus mortgage-backed securities held by foreign-related institutions posted a raw R-squared of 0.81, which in this project is roughly a religious experience. And unlike your typical shared-trend fireworks that evaporate the instant you detrend them, this one held onto a very respectable 0.44 after stripping out the time trends. That is the rare "huh, maybe something real is going on here" outcome -- both are big dollar-denominated financial balances that plausibly breathe with the same credit cycle, so I'll allow a single raised eyebrow. One. That said, please recall the ironclad house rule: these are randomly paired series, I am making no causal claim, and spurious correlation is the default setting of this entire operation. A strong number here means "pay attention," not "rearrange your portfolio around leased minivans." See you next week for more premium nonsense.
