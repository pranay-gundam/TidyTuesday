Welcome back to the weekly parade of numbers that have no business being in the same room together. This week our tireless little robot reached into the FRED grab-bag four times and pulled out pairs so unrelated that even a caffeine-deprived economist would raise an eyebrow. As always, absolutely nothing here is a real finding, a policy recommendation, or evidence of anything. It's spurious correlation all the way down, and that's the whole point.

Monday, August 18, gave us the crown jewel of trend-mirages: a discontinued index of hospital and nursing care output versus house prices in Nassau and Suffolk County, New York. The raw regression came in HOT, with an R-squared of 0.60 and a p-value tiny enough to make a grad student weep with joy. "Long Island home prices explained by nursing homes!" screams the headline I will not write. And then we detrend, and the whole thing evaporates like a puddle in July -- R-squared collapses to 0.08 and the p-value strolls up to a majestic 0.23. Turns out both series just went up over time, which, shocking, so did basically everything else in America. Nothing to see here except two lines that happened to climb the same staircase.

Tuesday's pairing of discontinued U.S. government grants against a mining output price deflator was, mercifully, a dud in both directions -- borderline in the raw run (p around 0.08, so barely worth a raised eyebrow) and completely limp once detrended (p of 0.53). Refreshingly honest of it to be boring at all times rather than pretending. Then Thursday, August 21, delivered the actual plot twist of the week: the share of mortgages held by the top 1 percent versus net government consumption of fixed capital on equipment and software. The raw regression was underwhelming, but the detrended version actually got STRONGER -- R-squared of 0.33 and a p-value of 0.005. That's the rare bird this project pretends to hunt for: a relationship that survives having its shared trend surgically removed. I have no theory for it beyond "rich people's mortgages and government software depreciation are apparently vibing," and I refuse to pretend otherwise.

Wednesday, August 20, matched a Canadian recession indicator (a series of ones and zeros, truly the pinnacle of data) against the San Francisco Tech Pulse, and remarkably it held significant in both the raw and detrended runs, with a negative coefficient suggesting tech sputters when Canada is in recession. It's a modest R-squared of around 0.07, so calm down, but it did clear the detrended bar, which is more than most contestants manage. File it under "mildly interesting, still almost certainly meaningless." As ever, please do not build a portfolio, a thesis, or a personality around any of this -- we are pairing random economic time series and watching what happens, and the correct reaction to all of it is a gentle chuckle. See you next week for more numbers behaving badly.
