\section{Weekly Summary}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10,colframe=black!50,title=AI-Generated Content]
\noindent The summary below was written by Claude (Anthropic) from that week's regression outputs and series descriptions. It has not been reviewed or fact-checked by a human, and should be read as an automated, informal recap -- not analysis.

Welcome back to the only newsletter brave enough to yank two economic time series out of a hat, mash them together, and act surprised at the results. This week the machine served up a delightfully lopsided pair of experiments, and I regret to inform you that one of them was actually kind of good, which ruins the whole vibe.

Let's start Monday off with the crowd favorite: the World Pandemic Uncertainty Index for New Zealand versus Total Private Construction Spending on Communication infrastructure in the United States. Yes, you read that correctly -- we asked whether Kiwis feeling nervous about pandemics has anything to do with how many dollars Americans throw at communication buildings. The raw regression came back with an R-squared of 0.008, which for the non-nerds means the relationship explains a heroic eight-tenths of one percent of anything, with a p-value of 0.32 that politely declined to be significant. And then, in a rare display of consistency, the detrended version also found nothing (p-value 0.45, R-squared basically a rounding error). So both versions agree: there is no there there. A relationship so weak it stayed weak even after we removed the trend. Truly the null hypothesis's finest hour. Frankly refreshing that the universe occasionally lets a nonsense pairing be nonsense in peace.

Tuesday, however, the algorithm got ambitious and paired two Consumer Price Indexes: core inflation (less food and energy) in the Northeast against Transportation Services inflation nationwide. And oh no, it's real. The raw regression posted an R-squared of 0.97, the kind of number that makes you check whether you accidentally regressed a series against itself. But here's the plot twist that this project usually lives to mock: even after ripping out each series' own time trend, the detrended relationship held up beautifully -- R-squared of 0.84, p-value in the neighborhood of ten-to-the-negative-twenty-three, which is science-speak for "we are very sure." So this is the rare, genuinely interesting case: two prices that move together not just because everything drifts upward over the decades, but because, well, they're both prices in the same economy responding to the same broad inflationary forces. Shocking. Inflation correlated with inflation. Alert the journals.

As always, let me remind you before anyone frames this newsletter and hangs it in a hedge fund lobby: these pairs are drawn at random, no theory, no hypothesis, no dignity. Spurious correlation is the house specialty here, not the exception, and even the one relationship that "held up" this week is two flavors of the same underlying thing rather than some daring discovery. So please do not go trading New Zealand pandemic anxiety futures based on American telecom construction. Enjoy the pretty scatterplots, distrust the pretty numbers, and I'll see you next week when the machine inevitably correlates cheese consumption with bond yields.
