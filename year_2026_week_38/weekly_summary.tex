Welcome back to another thrilling week of pointing a random-number cannon at the Federal Reserve's data warehouse and reporting whatever falls out. As always, none of these pairings mean anything, nobody chose them on purpose, and any relationship you see is roughly as meaningful as finding your birthday in the digits of pi. But we plotted it anyway, because that is the whole point of this deeply reasonable exercise.

Monday kicked us off with the House Price Index for Oxnard-Thousand Oaks-Ventura, California, valiantly squared off against a discontinued measure of financial-business equipment investment. The raw regression came out swinging with an R-squared of 0.87, which, if you didn't know better, would make you scream "EUREKA, Ventura housing IS financial equipment spending!" and immediately book a flight. Then we detrended it and the R-squared face-planted to 0.23. Translation: two things that both drifted upward for decades, high-fiving as they passed in the night. A textbook trend mirage, and honestly the exact species of nonsense this project exists to mock.

Tuesday's clash between San Luis Obispo house prices and a discontinued prime-loan-share series was the anti-climax of the century: not significant raw, not significant detrended, R-squared hovering around a rounding error. A rare instance of the data politely admitting it has nothing to say. Wednesday tried harder, pitting distillate fuel commercial CO2 emissions against Japanese retail stock surveys. The raw fit was a respectable 0.48, and here's the fun twist -- the detrended version stayed significant, and the sign flipped negative. So even after stripping out the shared drift, something wiggly refuses to fully die. Do I think carbon emissions from heating oil secretly whisper to Japanese retail inventory managers? Absolutely not. But I am contractually obligated to note it survived detrending, which makes it this week's most suspicious overachiever.

Thursday paired U.S. Assets against Asian business confidence, and both raw and detrended came back significant, though with coefficients so microscopic (and a scary condition-number warning waving its arms in the background) that I'd trust it about as far as I can throw a covariance matrix. Then Friday's finale: an emerging-markets corporate bond index versus the price of piped gas service. The raw regression looked gorgeous at R-squared 0.73, practically a soulmate situation -- until detrending dragged it down to 0.06 and rendered it gloriously insignificant, another romance that existed only because both lines happened to be walking uphill. So there you have it: a couple of trend-fueled flings, one genuinely stubborn oddball, and a whole lot of statistical theater. Please continue to draw zero conclusions, and I'll see you next week.
