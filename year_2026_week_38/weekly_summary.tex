\section{Weekly Summary}

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=yellow!10,colframe=black!50,title=AI-Generated Content]
\noindent The summary below was written by Claude (Anthropic) from that week's regression outputs and series descriptions. It has not been reviewed or fact-checked by a human, and should be read as an automated, informal recap -- not analysis.
\end{tcolorbox}

Welcome back to another week of shoving randomly selected economic time series into a blender and pretending the smoothie means something. As always, the machine dutifully paired up things that have no business being in the same sentence, ran the numbers twice, and left me to narrate the wreckage. Buckle up.

Monday kicked things off with a real crowd-pleaser: Oxnard-Thousand Oaks-Ventura house prices versus the gross fixed investment of financial businesses (a series so retired it's been discontinued since 2017). The raw regression came back with an R-squared of 0.87 and a p-value with more zeros than my bank account, which for one glorious moment made it look like California real estate and corporate equipment spending were soulmates. Then we detrended it, the R-squared face-planted to 0.23, and the "relationship" turned out to be two lines that both went up over several decades, like everything else in the economy. Classic trend mirage. Beautiful. Chef's kiss. This is the exact kind of result this project exists to point at and laugh.

Tuesday's San Luis Obispo house prices versus a discontinued loan-pricing series couldn't even manage a mirage — nothing significant raw, nothing significant detrended, just a shrug in numerical form. Wednesday, however, got weirdly interesting: distillate fuel commercial carbon emissions versus Japanese retail-trade stock surveys. The raw fit was a solid 0.48, and — plot twist — the detrended version actually stayed significant (p around 0.017), flipping to a negative relationship. So somewhere in the residuals, U.S. commercial heating-oil emissions and Japanese shopkeepers' feelings about their inventory are apparently doing a little dance together. I have absolutely no theory for this beyond "the universe is mocking me," but it earns the rare "huh, that held up" badge. Thursday delivered another survivor: U.S. Assets versus Asian business confidence held together in both the raw (barely, 0.08) and detrended (0.18, p around 0.0002) versions, which is genuinely more than most pairings manage — though the software did wave a giant "your numbers are enormous and possibly cursed" multicollinearity flag, so temper your excitement.

Friday gave us the week's tidiest cautionary tale: an emerging-markets corporate bond index versus the price of piped gas service. Raw R-squared of 0.73 with a triumphant near-zero p-value, then poof — detrended it collapsed to 0.06 and total insignificance. Two things drifting upward, holding hands, meaning nothing. To close us out, health-care "other separations" versus retail-trade hires was insignificant raw but crept into significance once detrended (p around 0.007), so file it under "mildly intriguing but probably still noise wearing a trench coat." The usual reminder, screamed from the rooftops: these are randomly paired series, there is no causal story here, and spurious correlation is not the bug — it is the entire point of the exhibit. See you next week for more statistically significant nonsense.
