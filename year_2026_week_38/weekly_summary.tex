\section{Weekly Summary}

Welcome back to the only weekly newsletter brave enough to shove two economic time series into a blender and call whatever comes out "science." This week the random number gods handed us a truly enthralling slate: California house prices versus some discontinued corporate spending figures, and then, for variety, more California house prices versus a discontinued measure of prime-based bank loans. If you were hoping for a coherent theme, I have terrible news and also I have never once had a coherent theme.

Monday's pairing was Oxnard-Thousand Oaks-Ventura house prices against "financial business gross fixed investment on equipment, software, and structures," a series so discontinued it stopped reporting back in 2017 and clearly took its dignity with it. The raw regression came out swinging with an R-squared of 0.87 and a p-value with more zeros than my bank account, which, if you took it at face value, would suggest that Ventura County home values and corporate gizmo-buying are practically soulmates. Then we detrended it, stripped out the shared "everything got bigger over fifty years" drift, and watched that beautiful 0.87 faceplant down to 0.23. So no, they are not soulmates; they just both happened to march upward through the decades like everything else in a growing economy. Classic trend mirage. This is the exact flavor of statistical heartbreak this project exists to point and laugh at.

Tuesday somehow managed to be even less impressive, which I did not think was possible. San Luis Obispo house prices versus the thrilling "percent of value of loans prime based by average loan size" gave us a raw R-squared of 0.039 -- yes, that is a three-point-nine percent, the statistical equivalent of a shrug -- and a p-value of 0.076 that couldn't even clear the traditional bar for pretend-significance. Detrended, it got worse, flipping the sign and slumping to a p-value of 0.14. Two series that never really agreed on anything, and after removing their trends they agreed even less. Somewhere a residual is filing for emotional distress.

As always, the mandatory reminder that I am legally and morally obligated to repeat: these are randomly paired series, chosen by a script, with absolutely no theory, causation, or rigor behind them. When something looks strong here, the smart money is on coincidence and shared drift, not on a hidden truth about the economy. This week's lesson, in case Monday didn't make it obvious enough: an R-squared of 0.87 can crumble into rubble the second you ask it to explain itself. Spurious correlation isn't the exception around here -- it's the whole business model.
