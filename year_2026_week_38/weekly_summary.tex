Welcome back to another thrilling installment of Randomly Yanking Two Time Series From FRED And Demanding They Explain Themselves. This week our tireless random-number generator paired up California house prices with discontinued financial investment flows, more California house prices with prime loan percentages, distillate fuel carbon emissions with Japanese retail stock sentiment, and U.S. assets with Asian business confidence. As always, none of these things have any earthly reason to be in the same room together, and yet here we are, running regressions and clutching our pearls at the results.

Monday kicked off with a bang: house prices in Oxnard-Thousand Oaks-Ventura versus some discontinued equipment-investment flow posted a raw R-squared of 0.87 and a p-value with seventy-five zeros of confidence behind it. Truly a relationship for the ages -- until you detrend it, at which point the R-squared face-plants down to 0.23. In other words, two things that both went up over several decades were shocked, shocked, to discover they'd both gone up. It survives detrending in the technical sense that the p-value is still small, but it lost two-thirds of its explanatory swagger the moment we took away its shared upward escalator. Classic trend-mirage energy, only partially refunded. Tuesday's San Luis Obispo house prices versus prime loan sizes was refreshingly honest by comparison: it wasn't significant raw and it wasn't significant detrended. A rare series that admits it has nothing to say.

Wednesday is where things got genuinely funny. Distillate fuel commercial carbon emissions versus Japanese retail stock sentiment showed a raw positive relationship (coefficient 1.21, R-squared 0.48) that, upon detrending, flipped its sign entirely and became a negative relationship that's still significant. So depending on whether you remove the trend, burning distillate fuel is either good for Japanese retail confidence or bad for it. This is the statistical equivalent of a magic 8-ball that answers differently based on how hard you shake it, and I refuse to build a policy around it. Thursday closed us out with U.S. Assets versus Asian business confidence, which pulled the same disappearing-and-reappearing act: barely-there negative raw slope, then a significant positive slope once detrended. It technically holds up, but its coefficient is measured in something like 0.0000003, a number so small it needs a hug.

The usual reminder, delivered with all the gravity of a man reading a cereal box aloud: these are randomly paired economic series, thrown together by a script that does not know or care what any of them mean. Nothing here is causal, nothing here is rigorous, and any relationship that "survives" is surviving in a lab where spurious correlation is the house specialty. If two numbers drift upward for fifty years and we act surprised they correlate, the joke is entirely on us -- which is, of course, the whole point. See you next week for more numbers pretending to know each other.
