Welcome back to the weekly ritual where we throw two unrelated economic series into a blender and solemnly report what sprays out. This week the dice gave us a genuinely mixed bag, which is to say: mostly noise, one absolute showstopper, and a couple of trend-mirages dressed up to look important. Grab a beverage.

Monday kicked us off with Belize's exchange rate versus the (discontinued, naturally) U.S. Balance on Services. The raw regression squeaked past the significance line with an R-squared of about nine percent, which is the statistical equivalent of a participation trophy. But here's the twist: once we detrended both series, the relationship didn't collapse -- it actually got slightly stronger and flipped sign. So Belizean currency and American services have a faint, stubborn little wiggle-for-wiggle thing going on that survives trend removal. Do I have the faintest idea why? Absolutely not. Am I going to pretend it means something? Only ironically.

Then Tuesday happened, and Tuesday was glorious. Gasoline prices in the South Census Region (Size Class B, because precision matters) versus 30-year FHA mortgage rates. The raw fit was decent, but the detrended version came back with an R-squared of 0.63 and a p-value with fifty-one zeros after the decimal point. That is, by the standards of this deeply unserious project, a tidal wave. A relationship this strong surviving detrending is actually the rare "huh, maybe this is real" case -- gas prices and mortgage rates both dance to the tune of inflation and the broader cost of money, so a genuine link here is at least theorizing-eligible rather than pure cosmic coincidence. I'll allow myself one (1) raised eyebrow.

The back half of the week brought us crashing back to earth, as is tradition. Wednesday paired corporate machinery cash deposits with government-sponsored enterprise debt, and the raw regression screamed an 80 percent R-squared -- which, the instant we detrended, evaporated into a limp, insignificant nothing. Textbook trend mirage: two numbers that both go up over time holding hands and calling it love. Thursday's Swedish government consumption prices versus Kansas City energy services told the same sad story (strong raw, dead on detrending), and Friday's corporate bond index against Middle Atlantic unemployment couldn't even muster a convincing fib -- the detrended p-value was a sleepy 0.79. As always, the standard disclaimer applies in bold invisible ink: these are randomly paired series, nothing here is causal, and spurious correlation isn't the exception, it's the entire business model.
