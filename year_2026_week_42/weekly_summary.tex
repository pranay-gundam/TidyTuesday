Welcome back to the weekly pilgrimage through the economic junk drawer, where we grab two time series that have never met, force them into a regression, and act shocked when they have opinions about each other. This week delivered a genuinely rare treat, a magnificent fraud, and one of the most beautifully plausible-looking lies the project has ever produced. Strap in.

Monday kicked us off with the Balance on Services (a discontinued U.S. trade statistic, because of course it is) versus the exchange rate of the Belize dollar per U.S. dollar. The raw regression squeaked out significance with a p-value of 0.045, which is the statistical equivalent of a result clearing the bar by tripping over it. But here's the twist nobody ordered: once we detrended both series, the relationship didn't collapse -- it actually got stronger, p dropping to 0.018 and even flipping sign. So after stripping out the shared march of time, U.S. services trade and the Belizean exchange rate still want to hold hands. Is there a deep economic story here? Almost certainly not. But this is the rarer, more interesting case the project pretends to care about, so we will dramatically raise one eyebrow and move on.

Tuesday was the showstopper. We paired average gasoline prices in the South Census Region, Size Class B (a level of specificity I find genuinely moving) against the 30-Year FHA Mortgage Rate. The raw regression was strong, R-squared around 0.17, p-value in the land of absurdly small. And then the detrended version didn't just survive -- it absolutely flexed, with an R-squared of 0.63 and a p-value with fifty-one zeros after the decimal point. Fifty-one. That is not a correlation, that is a marriage. Both gas prices and mortgage rates respond to the same underlying inflationary mood of the late 20th century, so there's an actual thread of economic sense here, which is deeply unsettling for a project built on nonsense. I may need to lie down.

Wednesday restored cosmic balance by handing us the textbook trend-mirage. Corporate machinery-sector cash holdings versus the debt liabilities of Government-Sponsored Enterprises posted a raw R-squared of 0.80 -- gorgeous, dazzling, the kind of number that gets you invited to parties. Then detrending took it out behind the shed: R-squared cratered to 0.02 and the p-value ballooned to 0.13, officially Not Significant and vaguely embarrassed. Both series just drift upward over the decades because everything denominated in dollars drifts upward over the decades, and once you remove that shared escalator, the "relationship" evaporates like my motivation on a Friday. This is exactly the kind of result this project lives to dunk on.

As always, the mandatory reminder: these pairs are chosen at random, nobody is claiming gasoline causes mortgages or that Belize is secretly steering American trade, and spurious correlation is the house specialty, not the exception. Two of this week's three "findings" either held up or imploded in exactly the ways statistics textbooks warn you about, and that's the whole dumb point. See you next week for more.
