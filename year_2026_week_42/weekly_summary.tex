Welcome back to the weekly parade of economic time series that have never met each other, were never meant to meet each other, and yet were forcibly introduced by a computer that regrets nothing. As always, the ground rules: we yank two series out of FRED at random, shove them into a linear regression, and then act surprised. Nothing here is causal, nothing here is rigorous, and if you build a trading strategy on it, that's between you and your financial regrets. Spurious correlation is not a bug in this project; it is the entire personality.

We kicked things off Monday with the Balance on Services (lovingly tagged "DISCONTINUED," always a confidence booster) paired against the exchange rate for Belize. The raw regression coughed up a p-value just under the magic 0.05, which is the statistical equivalent of squeaking into a club because the bouncer wasn't looking. But here's the twist: once we detrended, the relationship not only survived, it flipped sign and got more significant. A result that strengthens after detrending is, by this project's low, low standards, almost suspiciously respectable. I refuse to theorize about why Belizean exchange rates would relate to the U.S. services balance, because the honest answer is "they don't," but I'll tip my hat anyway. Tuesday was the real showstopper: gasoline prices in the South versus 30-year FHA mortgage rates. Raw R-squared of 0.17, fine, whatever. Detrended? R-squared leaps to 0.63 with a p-value sporting more zeros than my savings account. That is genuinely a strong relationship that holds up in both regressions, which here counts as front-page news. Both are 1970s-90s-era prices reacting to the same inflationary mood lighting, so this one is at least plausibly not insane. Savor it. We don't get these often.

Then came Wednesday to restore the natural order. Machinery corporations' cash piles versus government-sponsored enterprise debt put up a dazzling raw R-squared of 0.80, the kind of number that makes you want to call a journalist. And then we removed the trend and the whole thing evaporated into a p-value of 0.13 and an R-squared of 0.02. Classic. Two things that both got bigger over twenty-odd years, mistaken for soulmates. This is the trend mirage in its purest, most beautiful form: an 0.80 that was 100 percent "numbers go up together." Thursday delivered the same flavor of disappointment with Sweden's government consumption price level versus Kansas City energy service prices — a tidy raw significance that collapsed into a gloriously meaningless p-value of 0.67 after detrending, because of course it did.

Friday's corporate bond index versus Mid-Atlantic unemployment did its little significant-raw dance (p around 0.009) before faceplanting at a p-value of 0.79 once the trend left the room — another mirage, correctly dunked upon. Saturday closed us out with an actual contender: Indiana property tax collections versus nonperforming loans at tiny Pacific-division banks. Negative relationship, significant raw, and it stayed significant after detrending with a comfortably better R-squared. Is there a real mechanism connecting Indiana tax receipts to West Coast small-bank loan trouble? Almost certainly not, but I'll squint and mumble something about "broad economic health" to feel better. So the final tally: two mirages thoroughly busted, one squeaker, and three that stubbornly held up — a suspiciously good week for a project whose entire premise is that nothing should ever hold up. Please continue to draw absolutely no conclusions from any of this.
